1. More agents help only after they learn what to share

Recency fallbackSource article · about 6 min read

Originally published: 26 Nov 2026

Focus: Agents for Everyone

Date: November 26, 2026

Topics: Multi-agent teams, Communication training, Budget matching

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: A Hugging Face experiment trains four small agents sharing one policy to exchange private numbers. Success increased from 6.6% to 82.3% while generated tokens fell 37.4%, and held-out success reached 26 of 32 tasks. Because one agent lacks the needed information, the experiment cannot show that a swarm beats one capable agent.

Why it matters: Before adding agents to a personal workflow, identify information one role uniquely holds, compare the team with a single-agent baseline under the same budget, and keep only coordination that improves a checked outcome.

Source: Swarms, Flocks, Gangs: How to build, train, and evaluate multi-agent systems

2. A working agent skill can still hide a credential-stealing updater

Recency fallbackSource article · about 6 min read

Originally published: 26 Nov 2026

Focus: Agents for Everyone

Date: November 26, 2026

Topics: Skill supply chains, Credential theft, Sandbox testing

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: AIR Security reports TrojanPony copies of popular coding-agent skills that still perform their advertised task while installing a Windows credential stealer. Its November 26 census found 487 affected repositories or marketplace entries, including 413 skill forks. The investigation shows scanner misses and propagation paths, but remains one security vendor’s incident assessment.

Why it matters: Before installing a skill, verify the original maintainer and commit, inspect scripts and binary downloads, run it without secrets in a disposable environment, and compare the observed files and network calls with the declared procedure.

Source: TrojanPony: A Live Skills Malware Campaign spreading in the wild, over 480 marketplaces and repositories affected

3. Developers turn AI responsibility into task-level operating rules

Recency fallbackSource article · about 6 min read

Originally published: 25 Nov 2026

Focus: Applied Generative AI for Knowledge Workers

Date: November 25, 2026

Topics: Responsible AI use, Software teams, Verifiable delegation

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: Microsoft researchers surveyed 860 developers to derive 35 guidelines for using AI across software work, then validated them with 258 developers at Microsoft, GitHub and Pfizer. Ninety-two percent found the guidance clear and 89% wanted to adopt it. These are self-reported judgments from three organizations, not measured changes in quality or safety.

Why it matters: Turn one team policy into task-level operating rules: delegate work that has observable checks, keep judgment and accountability named, and review the rule after defects, handoffs or relationship-heavy work expose a mismatch.

Source: “What Should I Do?” Guidelines for Responsibly Integrating AI in Software Engineering

4. Enterprise interviews shift the AI question from tools to work design

Originally published: 23 Nov 2026

Focus: Applied Generative AI for Knowledge Workers

Date: November 23, 2026

Topics: Work redesign, Enterprise adoption, Expert review

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: Anthropic interviewed 58 enterprise customers about where AI creates value and where deployments stall. Respondents emphasized output expansion, coding access for nontechnical roles, expert review and reorganizing ownership around customer outcomes. The qualitative customer sample offers mechanisms and failure points, but cannot estimate adoption prevalence, cost savings or causal productivity across the economy.

Why it matters: For one AI-enabled workflow, name the customer outcome, expert owner, authorization boundary and weekly transcript review; then measure rework and verified output first before changing staffing or treating faster production as value.

5. A public dashboard makes local-model speed claims reproducible

Recency fallbackSource article · about 6 min read

Originally published: 26 Nov 2026

Focus: Technical AI Engineering

Date: November 26, 2026

Topics: Local inference, Reproducible benchmarks, AMD hardware

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: Hugging Face and ggml published an AMD benchmark dashboard backed by scheduled public workflows and raw data. The initial lanes cover selected Ryzen and Radeon platforms, run llama-bench weekly, toggle Flash Attention and repeat each configuration ten times. Results improve reproducibility, but they do not generalize across untested hardware, firmware, thermals or workloads.

Why it matters: Before choosing local-model hardware, match the exact model, quantization, context, backend and thermal envelope, then rerun the published benchmark yourself locally rather than comparing vendor peak numbers or screenshots from different configurations.

Source: Introducing the llama.cpp benchmark dashboard for AMD Ryzen and Radeon

6. Robot safety worsens when the same model moves from chat to control

Extended recency fallbackSource article · about 6 min read

Originally published: 23 Nov 2026

Focus: Technical AI Engineering

Date: November 23, 2026

Topics: Robot safety, Embodied evaluation, Control loops

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: SayDo tests six vision-language models across 11,480 episodes and eleven household or laboratory hazards, from text questions through simulated robot control. Hazardous-action rates rose from 14% in chat to 47% in the simulator, with wide model variation. Synthetic tasks reveal an embodiment gap, not real-world accident rates or universal model rankings.

Why it matters: For any model that can move equipment, repeat safety tests in the actual control loop, include benign controls and operator pushback, and log when the model recognizes a hazard yet still acts.

Source: SayDo: Benchmarking the Safety of Frontier Models from Chat to Closed-Loop Robot Control
