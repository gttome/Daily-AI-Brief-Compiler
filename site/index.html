1. Clocktower tests whether agents can coordinate, deceive and recover

Extended recency fallbackSource article · about 11 min read

Originally published: 05 Nov 2026

Focus: Agents for Everyone

Date: November 5, 2026

Topics: Multi-agent games, Deception testing, Long-horizon evaluation

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: Dwarkesh Patel describes Clocktower, an author-designed social-deduction benchmark played across 386 games using roughly $20,000 in API credits. Its roles require cooperation, conflicting private information, and sometimes deception. Reported Elo gaps and win rates measure performance inside this game setup; they do not establish general workplace reliability or deception risk.

Why it matters: Use adversarial role-play as one bounded stress test, then inspect transcripts for coordination failures, unsupported claims, and recovery behavior. Keep the game score separate from real-task evidence and require human approval for consequential actions.

2. A file-safe editing Skill puts recovery before replacement

Extended recency fallbackSource article · about 6 min read

Originally published: 05 Nov 2026

Focus: Agents for Everyone

Date: November 5, 2026

Topics: File-safe editing, Backup validation, Human approval

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: A draft, unverified incident record says a professor returned to find years of course files deleted after an AI-assisted editing request; Dropbox recovery reportedly restored them. The account cannot establish the exact causal chain. It nevertheless exposes a useful control gap: editing requests should not authorize deletion, replacement, or movement of original folders by default.

Why it matters: Package file editing as a reusable Skill that snapshots inputs, works only on copies, returns a diff and changed-file inventory, and pauses before replacement. Validate the backup and original inventory before approving any final move.

3. Google Workspace can encrypt sensitive files by policy

Extended recency fallbackSource article · about 6 min read

Originally published: 05 Nov 2026

Focus: Applied Generative AI for Knowledge Workers

Date: November 5, 2026

Topics: Policy encryption, Workspace governance, Confidential files

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: Google’s Policy-Based Enforcement beta can automatically apply client-side encryption to files matching admin rules across My Drive and shared drives. Encryption happens in place, preserving file URLs, and covers Workspace, Office, PDF, and image formats. The beta also adds user notices, audit records, and alerts when an external key service is unavailable.

Why it matters: Eligible teams should test one narrowly scoped rule against nonproduction files, confirm key-service failure alerts, and inspect user notices and audit logs. The beta requires Assured Controls licensing and allowlisting, so availability and operational recovery need verification before broader rollout.

4. Australia’s central bank maps an uncertain AI productivity curve

New developmentSource article · about 10 min read

Originally published: 09 Nov 2026

Focus: Applied Generative AI for Knowledge Workers

Date: November 9, 2026

Topics: Productivity J-curve, Workforce evidence, Adoption planning

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: Reserve Bank of Australia economist Sarah Hunter says AI adoption is fast but aggregate productivity effects remain difficult to measure. She cites a survey where 35% of adopters reported no productivity gain, while 8% were reducing headcount and 6% increasing it. The speech presents estimates ranging from 0.1 to 2 percentage points.

Why it matters: Build an adoption case around measured task time, quality, training, and redesign costs instead of a single economy-wide forecast. Track employment changes separately from productivity, and revisit the baseline as comparable observations accumulate.

5. A saved coding-agent approval exposed real secrets

Extended recency fallbackSource article · about 8 min read

Originally published: 05 Nov 2026

Focus: Technical AI Engineering

Date: November 5, 2026

Topics: Command approvals, Secret exposure, Repository hygiene

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: Apiiro reports finding 2,539 real secrets across 1,994 public repositories after coding-agent approvals stored exact commands in local project settings. In 1,122 of 104,185 public Claude settings files, its scanners found a real secret. The company says automatic ignore rules help only when the file was created normally and never force-added.

Why it matters: Audit saved agent commands for embedded credentials, rotate exposed keys, and keep local permission files out of version control. Replace broad permanent approvals with narrow command patterns, then test repository checks against an intentionally harmless sample secret.

6. Nous publishes a 27B model and its political-framing evals

Recency fallbackSource article · about 9 min read

Originally published: 06 Nov 2026

Focus: Technical AI Engineering

Date: November 6, 2026

Topics: Open model weights, Framing evaluation, Evaluator design

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: Nous Research released Arete-27B, a post-trained Qwen3.8-27B model, with weights, training references, and an 803-prompt evaluation spanning seven political and cultural categories. The publisher designed the test and reports the comparative results, so its labels and conclusions require independent scrutiny rather than being treated as a neutral measure of model quality.

Why it matters: Re-run a small, balanced subset with blinded raters before adopting the publisher’s framing labels. Inspect prompt wording, refusal behavior, and evaluator agreement, then compare task performance so a political preference test does not substitute for general capability evidence.
