1. Agent pilots start with workflow evidence, not ambition

Extended recency fallbackSource article · about 5 min read

Originally published: 12 Nov 2026

Focus: Agents for Everyone

Date: November 12, 2026

Topics: Workflow redesign, Bounded pilots, Human approval

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: Microsoft argues that organizations get more value by redesigning complete workflows than by adding isolated AI features. Its customer examples are vendor-reported, so they should guide questions rather than prove outcomes. The practical lesson is to define the baseline, decision rights and measurable result before an agent is introduced into everyday work.

Why it matters: Pick one noncoding workflow, record its current time and error baseline, map every handoff, and define the agent’s allowed actions. Run a bounded trial with human approval, then compare results and document which vendor claims remain unverified.

2. Recursive self-improvement depends on inspectable assumptions

Recency fallbackSource article · about 6 min read

Originally published: 13 Nov 2026

Focus: Agents for Everyone

Date: November 13, 2026

Topics: Self-improvement loops, Bottleneck analysis, Uncertainty

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: Dwarkesh Patel examines recursive self-improvement as a chain of assumptions about research automation, feedback quality, compute and the ability to improve the systems doing the research. The essay does not establish a timetable. Its value is a structured map of bottlenecks and uncertainty that decision-makers can test instead of treating “self-improvement” as one event.

Why it matters: When evaluating recursive-improvement claims, list each required step and the evidence supporting it. Mark unknown feedback, compute and evaluation constraints separately, then define what new observation would change your decision rather than debating one all-or-nothing forecast.

4. Procurement records expose an H200 compliance gap

Extended recency fallbackSource article · about 6 min read

Originally published: 11 Nov 2026

Focus: Applied Generative AI for Knowledge Workers

Date: November 11, 2026

Topics: Chip procurement, Export controls, Evidence review

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Independent Reporting
Availability: Available

Summary: Reuters found procurement records showing a PLA-linked Chinese university bought two Nvidia H200 chips for 874,700 yuan and separately rented H200 access despite United States restrictions. The records establish contracting activity, not confirmed delivery, chip origin or end use. That distinction matters when compliance teams interpret sanctions and supplier evidence.

Why it matters: For restricted AI hardware, separate purchase records from delivery, provenance and actual use. Preserve the original tender, supplier identity, payment terms and access logs, then route unresolved origin or end-user questions to legal review before making a compliance claim.

5. WikiTrace links agent memory back to evidence

Extended recency fallbackSource article · about 6 min read

Originally published: 10 Nov 2026

Focus: Technical AI Engineering

Date: November 10, 2026

Topics: Agent memory, Evidence links, Long-horizon tasks

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: WikiTrace introduces an evidence-linked memory system for long-running language-model agents. Instead of storing conclusions alone, it preserves insights, rationales and trajectories, then retrieves them across five agent benchmarks. The authors report gains over prior memory methods and over a same-insights baseline, while identifying and reducing memory-induced performance degradation.

Why it matters: When testing agent memory, retain the source evidence and reasoning path beside each stored insight. Compare against both no-memory and same-insight baselines, then log cases where retrieved memories degrade decisions instead of assuming every retained trace helps.

6. A trust pyramid turns generated code into evidence

Extended recency fallbackSource article · about 5 min read

Originally published: 11 Nov 2026

Focus: Technical AI Engineering

Date: November 11, 2026

Topics: Code verification, Trust pyramid, Runtime evidence

Open the permanent story page

Evidence: Publisher Authored
Availability: Available

Summary: Lauren Tan argues that generated code should earn trust through layered evidence rather than style or authorship. Her trust pyramid moves from architecture and type constraints through linting and tests to runtime verification. The framework separates aesthetic objections from observable failures, giving teams a repeatable way to review both human and machine-written changes.

Why it matters: Define required evidence before accepting generated code: architectural boundaries, type checks, lint rules, tests and runtime observations. Record which layer caught each defect, and treat persuasive formatting or familiar style as weak signals unless supported by executable verification.
